Evangelizing a Design System

MyHeritage had grown into a 500-person powerhouse with dozens of genealogy products. As home DNA kits surged in popularity, we saw an opportunity: combine genetic data with family history to unlock powerful health insights—and break into health tech.

We hired scientists, launched research, and dove into FDA approvals. With a hard deadline and a race to be first-to-market, we moved fast, reusing whatever we could from existing products.

That urgency sparked the long and winding road to the company's first-ever Design System.

The Team

In my position at the time as Director of Product Design, I initiated and led the two-year process. What began with guiding two Senior Product Designers on the Health product, evolved into a full company rebrand and Design System development, involving our entire Design team of eight Product Designers, two Marketing Designers, two external agencies, and close collaboration with Product, Marketing, and R&D.

The Challenge

As with many large organizations with deep product legacy, I had to overcome the familiar challenges of building a new Design System: driving management buy-in by raising awareness to the current problems and the great possible long term value, assembling a dedicated team, rolling out new tools and workflows, leading company-wide training, integrating the system into the roadmap with clear KPIs, and continuously improving it all.

But a few steps back, to where it all started, designing the new Health product...

Brand Brothers

As a product that will be an extension of the current ancestry DNA kit services, it was clear that the brands should be related, creating a coherent product offering. As such, the entire visual language, the logo, the physical kit and all marketing materials were developed further, given a more scientific and trust-worthy feel, yet keeping alignment and communication between the two products.

Reuse, Reuse, Reuse

From a Product Design perspective, I pushed the team into reusing existing designs and behaviours, allowing R&D to build everything as fast as possible. In addition to the brand ties, the DNA product was the most up-to-date product, from both design and code perspectives, so we decided to build on that.

All UX behaviours, page structures, basic components, iconography, typography and style standards, were mapped and organized, to be easily reused, and expanded when needed, as we were building the very first building blocks of our repository. The brand’s different primary color was the only differentiation between the two products.

However, In parallel, many marketing and visual materials were being made for the upcoming release, some in external agencies, and the lack of a clear brand strategy and brand guidelines was hurting us.

The inconsistency between the three products was rough...

The MyHeritage Story

It wasn’t easy stopping the many moving parts that were in motion, but I realized that we had to stop and create a coherent brand strategy, a story that connects the three main product domains. We collaborated with our Marketing and Business Development departments, and after a few workshops and different directions that were tested, we finalized the brand story:
Empowering users to connect their past (Genealogy), present (DNA), and future (Health).

With this decision in mind, I gathered the design team for a branding offsite, that stretched across a few days of endless brainstorming, sketching, collaborating, debating, and agreeing. We finally locked into our preferred direction, and clear values were defined for each domain: the warmth and familyhood of the genealogy products, the diversity and excitement of the DNA product, and the scientific, humane empowerment of the health product. These values will be represented by playful sceneries, combining fitting cut-out imagery, lively illustrations, and our dominant brush stroke element. The current primary brand colors will be kept as an important differentiator between the domains, and a new cross-domain wide color palette will be added.

Being analogical in a digital world

Genealogy is a deeply nostalgic domain—rooted in old documents, photos, and archives that offer glimpses into the lives of our ancestors. In contrast to the more modern feel of our DNA and Health products, we wanted this space to carry a subtle distinction, emphasizing that analog, nostalgic charm. We incorporated vintage objects into the icons and illustrations of this domain, creating the desired distinction and twist we were looking for.

I just had to animate some of these as well...

Next Steps

As the brand solidified and clear guidelines were set for the upcoming Health product, R&D was moving quickly. The initial foundations we laid—around component reuse and ux alignment—were already proving their value, and management was surprised by the pace of development. Suddenly, terms like ‘Design Systems’ and ‘Components’ were echoing through the hallways (helped by my team and me whispering them at every opportunity). I saw this as the perfect moment to reignite a Design System initiative—something we had attempted before, but never quite got off the ground.

I dove deeper into our product design audit, mapping, aligning, and specifying all shared structures, layouts, components, behaviors, and atomic elements—those already in use or with the potential to be—based on current and anticipated needs, our initial guidelines, and industry standards.

Next, I asked my team to test these decisions on some of our core, older genealogy products, including the family tree editor, the historical records search engine, and the automatic matches feature. The results were promising.

I involved R&D from the very beginning, making sure they felt like key contributors in the process by recognizing their importance, input, and ownership. Their enthusiasm and commitment to driving this initiative forward was a game changer. Additionally, I aimed for our code and design repositories to be fully aligned and code-based. This made close collaboration on component structure, props, and responsiveness essential. The smart, content-aware, responsive, and interactive components created in Figma were handed off to R&D, who built them in React while documenting everything in Storybook.

We kept on diving deeper into the process, as more complex and company specific components and specs were being added to the Design System.

Resolution

In parallel, new features were being developed across multiple product domains. Management noticed how fast and consistently things were being designed, built, and shipped—and R&D efficiency graphs made it clear: the Design System, though still young, was already making a strong impact.

But over time, that impact began to fade. Limited resources, tight deadlines, and packed roadmaps took over. Our Storybook repository became cluttered with half-built, duplicated components. Slight variations were being rebuilt from scratch, and local overrides slowed us down and made maintenance a challenge. The gap between Figma and Storybook kept growing, as R&D struggled to keep up with design deliverables.

We found ourselves chasing our own tail, trying to bring order to a system slipping out of sync. I knew it was time to rethink our processes and reallocate resources to get things back on track. To make the case, I proposed a comparison between Figma and Storybook—and the result was striking: only 50% of the Design System had actually been implemented.
It was clear: things had to change.

What began as a short technical sprint with all front-end developers to close major gaps quickly, evolved into a lasting structural shift—one I had been advocating for over the years: dedicated front-end Design System resources. While we didn’t form a unified team, each product domain committed a few team members to focus primarily on the Design System. We established clear standards for what each component must include, banned overrides and duplication, and set up strong communication and documentation practices.

Since then, we’ve continued refining the process. Two years in, the company now operates with a well-structured, decentralized Design System model—fully embedded in product goals and roadmaps. It’s actively maintained and scaled as the company grows. The results have been transformative: faster time-to-market, greater UX consistency, and smoother collaboration across teams. The Design System was instrumental in launching viral AI Avatars and Deep Learning features at record speed—helping us stay ahead of the curve.

What a journey it’s been.
